# Does It Snow in Vancouver? A Property Owner's Winter Weather Guide

Yes — it snows in Vancouver. Not as often as Toronto or Calgary, and rarely for weeks at a time, but every winter Metro Vancouver gets at least one accumulating snow event, and roughly one winter in four delivers a multi-day storm that shuts down transit, ices sidewalks for a week, and generates thousands of slip-and-fall claims. If you own or manage property in Greater Vancouver, "we don't really get snow here" is the most expensive assumption you can make.

## How Much Snow Does Vancouver Actually Get?

According to Environment and Climate Change Canada's climate normals for Vancouver International Airport (YVR), the city averages roughly **38 cm of snow per year**, spread across about 10 snow-days. That's the coastal, sea-level average — and it's misleading in three important ways:

- **The North Shore gets 2–4× more.** North Vancouver, West Vancouver, and any address above 150 m elevation regularly clear over 100 cm per season.
- **Burnaby, Coquitlam and the Tri-Cities get 1.5–2× more.** Capitol Hill, Burnaby Mountain, Burke Mountain, Heritage Mountain, and Westwood Plateau all sit in a colder microclimate than downtown Vancouver.
- **The average hides the outliers.** Winters like 2016–17, 2021–22 and 2008–09 delivered 60–100 cm in a single week to the city floor. One severe winter every 3–5 years is the actual planning baseline.

## When Does It Snow in Vancouver?

Vancouver's snow season runs **mid-November through mid-March**, with the highest probability of an accumulating event between **late December and mid-February**. Specific patterns to know:

- **Arctic outflow events.** When a cold high-pressure system settles over the BC interior, freezing air spills down the Fraser Valley and through the Sea-to-Sky corridor. These events are cold, dry and windy — perfect for snow accumulation and drift-loading on parkade ramps.
- **Pineapple express flip.** A Pacific atmospheric river arriving on the back of an arctic outflow produces the most dangerous Vancouver weather: rain falling on a frozen surface. Every major freezing-rain event in Vancouver history has followed this pattern.
- **Overnight sub-zero, daytime melt.** The classic Vancouver freeze-thaw cycle. Snow falls at 11 p.m., melts to 3 cm of slush by 2 p.m., refreezes to a sheet of ice by 6 p.m. This is when most slip-and-falls happen.

## Vancouver's Worst Winters On Record

For property owners, the memory that matters is not the average — it's the outlier. Recent examples:

- **2021–22:** A late-December Arctic outflow dumped 40+ cm on Vancouver over 72 hours, followed by two weeks of sub-zero temperatures. TransLink partially suspended service. Insurance industry sources reported record slip-and-fall claim volumes.
- **2016–17:** The "snowiest Vancouver winter since 1996." Over 65 cm at YVR across the season, with a 20 cm single event in early February. Roof-collapse warnings issued for older commercial buildings.
- **2008–09:** 100+ cm across the season at YVR. Multi-week snow cover, dozens of strata corporations hit with liability claims from unshovelled walkways.
- **1996–97:** The reference winter. 65 cm in a single 48-hour December storm. Vancouver was effectively immobilized for a week.

The pattern: a severe Vancouver winter arrives every 3–5 years. Planning for the average and getting caught by the outlier is the single most common property-management mistake in the Lower Mainland.

## What Vancouver Snow Actually Does to Property

Vancouver snow behaves differently than prairie snow. Two key characteristics:

1. **It's wet and heavy.** Coastal snow has 2–3× the water content of Calgary or Winnipeg snow. That means heavier shovel loads, faster ice formation on refreeze, and — for older commercial buildings — real roof-load stress after a 20+ cm event.
2. **It transitions fast.** A Vancouver snow event routinely flips to rain, then to freezing rain, then back to snow, within a single 24-hour window. The freeze-thaw cycle is what generates the slip-and-fall claims, not the snowfall itself.

That combination is why Vancouver salt-application volumes per snow-cm are typically higher than in colder Canadian cities — the freeze-thaw window makes reapplication mandatory.

## Legal Responsibilities for Vancouver Property Owners

The City of Vancouver **Street and Traffic By-law** requires the owner or occupier of every property to clear snow and ice from the public sidewalks adjacent to their property by **10 a.m. the day after a snowfall**. Every neighbouring municipality (Burnaby, Richmond, North Vancouver, West Vancouver, Coquitlam, Surrey, New Westminster, Port Moody, Port Coquitlam) has a functionally identical by-law with a similar deadline.

For strata corporations, that obligation extends across all common-property walkways, parkade ramps, visitor stalls and fire lanes. For commercial property owners it covers your entire parking lot, every storefront sidewalk, every loading bay and every accessible-parking aisle. Failure to clear is a bylaw-ticket exposure; the far bigger risk is **occupier's liability** for a slip-and-fall injury, which in BC courts routinely produces six-figure settlements.
