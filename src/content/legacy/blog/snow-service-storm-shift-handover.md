## Start with what changed

A snow-service handover should tell the next person what they cannot safely assume. A cleared entrance may have received fresh accumulation, an access gate may have been locked, or a delivery may have changed the usable space. A short, dated update is more useful than a message saying the property is done.

For a strata or commercial property, choose one place for operational updates and identify the person who can confirm instructions. Keep residents’ personal information and access codes out of public posts and shared marketing photographs.

## Separate completed work from observations

Record the service area, the approximate visit time and what was actually completed. Then record observations separately: an inaccessible stair, standing water, a vehicle blocking a route or a section requiring a follow-up decision. An observation is not a diagnosis, and a photograph does not prove that a surface will remain clear.

Use simple status labels such as completed, unable to access and follow-up requested. Avoid a single green check mark for the entire property when the scope contains several different routes and surfaces.

## Make the next action explicit

Each open item needs an owner and a next step. That could be asking the property manager to arrange vehicle access, confirming whether another visit is requested, or asking the coordinator to review a change in conditions. State which items are awaiting authorization.

A useful handover includes the site identifier, agreed priority routes, remaining constraints, the next contact and any change to the original plan. It should not invent a response time or authorize additional work outside the agreement.

## Keep a practical record

Use factual descriptions that someone unfamiliar with the shift can follow. Retain original timestamps and distinguish an operator report from a manager’s later comment. Store private site records in the agreed internal system rather than in an open chat link.

WorkSafeBC’s guidance on preventing slips and falls on icy surfaces is useful background for winter awareness. A handover supports coordination; it is not a substitute for assessing conditions or managing a hazard at the site.

## Bring the handover into the service brief

Before the season, ask how visit notes and changing site conditions will be communicated. Confirm who receives updates, who acts overnight and how occupants can report a problem without sending instructions to several different crews.
