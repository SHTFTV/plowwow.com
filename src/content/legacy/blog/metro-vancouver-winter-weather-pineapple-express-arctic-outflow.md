# Metro Vancouver Winter Weather — How Pineapple Express and Arctic Outflow Trigger Snow Emergencies

The reason Metro Vancouver's winters look calm on paper and chaotic in practice is that we don't get one kind of winter storm — we get four. A Kitsilano strata driveway can be dry and 8 °C on a Tuesday, glazed over with freezing rain by Wednesday 5 a.m., buried under 22 cm of Fraser Valley outflow snow on Thursday, and back to bare pavement by Friday afternoon. Understanding *which* pattern is coming determines what a snow contractor should be doing tonight. **PlowWow** watches the same forecast models Environment Canada uses — GDPS, HRDPS, ECMWF and the RDPA — and this guide translates those patterns into what actually happens on your driveway, sidewalk or loading apron.

## The Four Metro Vancouver Winter Patterns

Every operational snow decision we make in Metro Vancouver is triggered by one of the following four synoptic setups. They are meaningfully different from each other and each requires a different response.

- **Pineapple Express / atmospheric river.** Warm moist flow from the sub-tropical Pacific. Heavy rain at sea level, heavy wet snow at 400+ metres, freezing rain in the transition band. Named storms in this class have delivered 100–200 mm of rain to Metro Vancouver in 24 hours.
- **Arctic outflow.** Cold, dry air pushed south out of the BC interior through the Fraser Valley and coastal fiords. Temperatures drop 10–20 °C in 24 hours. Little precipitation at first — but when a Pacific system arrives on the trailing edge, that cold air pool converts the moisture into heavy snow at sea level.
- **Convergence-zone snow.** Winds split around the Olympic Peninsula and reconverge over Metro Vancouver, generating localized heavy snow bands over Surrey, Delta and the southern part of the Lower Mainland while Vancouver proper stays clear. Notoriously hard to forecast — models under-resolve the band.
- **Freezing-rain / glaze events.** Warm air aloft, cold surface. A layer of above-freezing air overrides a shallow sub-zero surface layer, producing rain that freezes on contact. The single highest-risk pattern for slip-and-fall claims because it happens fast and looks like wet asphalt.

## Pineapple Express — What It Actually Does to Property

The Pineapple Express is famous for flood risk, but its snow-and-ice consequences are just as expensive. The pattern typically arrives with the following signature at strata and commercial sites:

- **Elevation split.** Heavy rain at sea level. Vancouver's West End might see 60 mm of rain while West Vancouver's Cypress and British Properties see 40 cm of wet snow. Same storm, same day, very different sites.
- **Heavy wet snow at 300–500 metres.** North Vancouver benches, Burnaby Mountain, Burke Mountain, Coquitlam's Westwood Plateau and Chilliwack's Promontory Heights all get hit while flat neighbourhoods stay bare.
- **Freezing-rain sandwich.** The leading edge of a Pineapple Express often produces 4–12 hours of freezing rain on the coast before the warm sector arrives. This is the highest-liability window of the entire winter.
- **Roof and gutter loading.** Wet snow at 900+ kg per cubic metre stresses low-slope commercial roofs and strata parkade slabs.

Operational response: **pre-treat surfaces before the leading edge, be on-site for the freezing-rain window, and stay on-site through the warm-sector transition.** Every Pineapple Express is a two- to three-day operation, not a one-morning push.

## Arctic Outflow — The Fraser Valley's Signature Winter

Arctic outflow events form when a strong high-pressure ridge builds over the BC interior and the Yukon, forcing dense cold air southward through the Fraser Valley's natural funnel. The pattern is straightforward once you know the signature:

- **Winds accelerate through Hope, Chilliwack and Abbotsford** and fan out across Langley, Surrey, Delta, Burnaby and Coquitlam. Gusts to 80 km/h are common.
- **Temperatures drop hard.** From +6 °C to -12 °C in 24 hours is typical. The record differential exceeds 25 °C in 36 hours.
- **Little snow at first.** Arctic outflow itself is dry. But the moment a Pacific low approaches the coast, the collision produces the deep-snow events Metro Vancouver remembers: December 2008, January 2017, December 2021, January 2024.
- **Wind chill.** Apparent temperatures of -20 to -25 °C in the Fraser Valley change the operational picture — MgCl₂ under-performs, and CaCl₂ becomes the correct de-icer.

Operational response: **switch de-icer product, extend salt-run frequency, and pre-position equipment in the eastern Fraser Valley before the storm arrives from the west.**

## Convergence-Zone Snow — The One That Catches Everyone

Convergence-zone events are notoriously hard to forecast because the models under-resolve the interaction of the Olympic Peninsula terrain with the flow across the Strait of Juan de Fuca. In practice:

- Wind splits around the Olympics and reconverges anywhere from Nanaimo to Bellingham.
- When the band sets up over the southern Lower Mainland (Delta, Surrey, White Rock, Langley), those areas can pick up 10–30 cm while Vancouver proper stays dry.
- Bands are narrow — a strata in South Surrey might get 20 cm while a strata 8 kilometres north gets 2 cm.
- Duration is short (4–8 hours) but intensity is high.

Operational response: **do not trust a single forecast point.** PlowWow's dispatch monitors Doppler radar returns in real time during suspected convergence-zone setups and moves crews southeast pre-emptively.

## Freezing-Rain / Glaze — The Highest-Liability Pattern

Freezing rain is a coastal-BC specialty because we sit at the exact latitude and elevation where warm-air overrides and cold-surface layers routinely coexist. The pattern that produces most Metro Vancouver freezing-rain events:

- A shallow Arctic outflow leaves a sub-zero layer at the surface (0–200 m elevation).
- A warm Pacific system slides over the top, producing rain aloft.
- Raindrops fall through the sub-zero surface layer and freeze on contact with pavement.
- The result is a glass-clear layer of ice, often only 1–3 mm thick, on top of surfaces that look wet.

**This is the pattern that produces the highest slip-claim frequency of the winter.** Every operational SOP at PlowWow treats freezing rain differently than snow: crews pre-treat with liquid brine 4–8 hours ahead of the leading edge, apply higher-concentration de-icer during the event, and return every 90 minutes until the surface transitions to bare and dry.

## The Snow Days Metro Vancouver Actually Remembers

For calibration, the events that made the Environment Canada climate records:

- **December 2008.** Multi-week deep-cold outflow followed by a Pacific low. Vancouver International Airport recorded 74 cm of snow in December.
- **February 2019.** Successive convergence-zone snowfalls. Surrey and Langley picked up 40+ cm over 10 days while Vancouver stayed under 15 cm.
- **January 2020.** A cold outflow followed by a Pineapple Express produced freezing rain, then heavy snow, then a rapid warm-up in 72 hours.
- **December 2021.** Arctic-outflow event with -20 °C wind chills in the Fraser Valley followed by a Pacific low delivered 25 cm to Chilliwack and Abbotsford.
- **January 2024.** Freezing-rain event across the entire Lower Mainland; single highest slip-claim frequency in the Metro Vancouver insurance market for the decade.

Every one of these events was a multi-day operation. A snow contractor priced for "average" Metro Vancouver winters will be materially exposed if the season delivers a 2008, 2020 or 2024-class event.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often does Metro Vancouver actually get snow?**
Long-term climate normals show 10–20 pushable snow events per winter across the Lower Mainland, with a factor-of-six range between mild and severe seasons.

**Which pattern is worst for freezing rain?**
The transition zone between a departing Arctic outflow and an arriving Pineapple Express is the classic freezing-rain setup for Metro Vancouver.

**Why is Fraser Valley snow so much heavier than Vancouver snow?**
Because arctic outflow keeps the Fraser Valley colder for longer, so when Pacific moisture arrives the sub-zero surface layer holds. Sea-level Vancouver often transitions to rain first.

**Do convergence-zone events really only hit south of the Fraser?**
Not always, but the most common band setup produces heavy snow across Surrey, Delta, White Rock and Langley while Vancouver proper stays clear.

**How far out can PlowWow forecast a snow event?**
Pattern reliability is very good at 3–5 days for the Pineapple Express and Arctic outflow patterns, moderate at 2–3 days for convergence zones, and only 6–12 hours for freezing-rain onset.
